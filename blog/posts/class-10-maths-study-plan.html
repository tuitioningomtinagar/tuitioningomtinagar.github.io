Class 10 Maths Study Plan: A Practical Guide for Students

Published: October 5, 2026 · Written and reviewed by: Anand

About the author

This article is written and reviewed by Anand, who holds a BCA and has 6 years of professional IT experience along with 2 years of teaching experience.

The study suggestions in this article are intended as practical guidance. Students should adapt the schedule according to their school syllabus, current preparation level and examination timetable.

Class 10 Maths becomes much easier to manage when students stop treating the entire syllabus as one large task. A better approach is to divide the work into smaller topics, practise regularly, keep track of mistakes and revise the material repeatedly.

The exact amount of time a student needs will depend on their current level, school schedule and other subjects. The goal of a study plan should therefore be consistency rather than simply studying for a large number of hours.

This guide provides a practical framework that students can adjust to their own timetable.

1. Assess your current level first

Before creating a timetable, find out where you currently stand. Look at recent school tests, homework and classwork. Identify topics where you can solve questions independently and topics where you regularly need help.

Try dividing topics into three simple groups:

  • Comfortable: You can solve most questions without significant help.
  • Needs practice: You understand the concept but make mistakes or take too long to solve questions.
  • Needs help: You are not yet comfortable with the underlying concept.

This simple classification can help you decide where to spend more of your study time.

2. Divide the syllabus into manageable parts

Avoid trying to complete several difficult topics in one sitting. Break your syllabus into smaller targets.

For example, instead of writing "finish Maths" in your timetable, write a specific target such as:

  • Understand the concept.
  • Review solved examples.
  • Solve basic questions.
  • Solve application-based questions.
  • Review mistakes.

A smaller target is easier to measure and makes it easier to see whether you are actually progressing.

3. Create a daily Maths routine

Regular practice is generally more useful than studying Maths for a long period only once or twice a week.

A student might structure a study session like this:

  • 10 minutes: Review formulas or previous mistakes.
  • 20 minutes: Understand or revise the day's concept.
  • 30 minutes: Solve practice questions.
  • 10 minutes: Check answers and record mistakes.

This is only an example. Students with more or less available time can adjust the duration while keeping the same basic structure.

A weekly plan can help balance new learning, practice and revision.

The plan can be changed around school tests, holidays and other subjects. What matters most is maintaining a regular cycle of learning, practice and revision.

5. Practise Maths by solving, not just reading

Reading a solution can make a question appear easy even when you cannot solve it independently. For that reason, students should spend a significant part of their Maths study time actually solving problems.

When you get stuck, avoid immediately looking at the complete solution. First identify exactly where the difficulty occurs. Then review the relevant concept and try the problem again.

After seeing a solution, close the book and attempt the question again yourself. This helps distinguish between understanding a solution and being able to produce one.

6. Keep a Maths mistake notebook

One of the simplest ways to improve is to keep a record of mistakes.

The notebook does not need to contain every incorrect answer. Focus on mistakes that are repeated or reveal a gap in understanding.

For each mistake, write:

  • The question or topic.
  • What went wrong.
  • The correct method.
  • What you should remember next time.

Review this notebook once or twice a week.

7. Use spaced revision instead of one-time revision

Completing a chapter once does not mean that it is permanently remembered. Return to important concepts several times.

A simple pattern could be:

  • Review shortly after learning the topic.
  • Practise again a few days later.
  • Test yourself the following week.
  • Revisit difficult questions during monthly revision.

The exact schedule can vary, but regular retrieval and practice can help you identify what you actually remember.

8. When should you solve sample papers?

Sample papers become more useful after you have covered a substantial portion of the syllabus. Early in the year, focus more heavily on understanding concepts and practising individual topics.

As examinations approach, start combining topics and solving papers under conditions similar to the actual examination.

Do not simply check your final score. Analyse the paper afterwards.

  • Which questions did you leave incomplete?
  • Where did you make calculation errors?
  • Which concepts were unclear?
  • Did you spend too much time on a particular question?
  • Were your steps and presentation clear?

9. When might additional tuition help?

Some students can prepare effectively through school lessons, self-study and regular practice. Others may need additional support when they repeatedly struggle with particular concepts.

Additional tuition may be worth considering if a student:

  • Has difficulty understanding basic concepts.
  • Repeatedly makes the same type of mistake.
  • Is unable to maintain a regular study routine independently.
  • Needs someone to explain a topic in a different way.
  • Needs structured practice and feedback.

Tuition should complement the student's own effort rather than replace independent practice.

A practical teaching perspective

From a teaching perspective, one useful starting point is to identify exactly where a student is getting stuck rather than simply assigning more questions. Once the difficulty is identified, practice can be focused on that particular concept or skill.

Students also benefit from reviewing their own mistakes. Understanding why an answer was incorrect can be more useful than simply moving on to the next question.

A simple weekly Maths routine

If you are unsure where to begin, this simple routine can be adapted to your school timetable:

  • Monday: Learn one concept and solve basic questions.
  • Tuesday: Continue practice and review Monday's mistakes.
  • Wednesday: Work on another topic.
  • Thursday: Solve mixed questions.
  • Friday: Revise formulas and difficult areas.
  • Saturday: Complete a longer practice session.
  • Sunday: Take a short self-test and review the week's mistakes.

Common mistakes students should avoid

  • Studying only immediately before a test.
  • Reading solutions without attempting questions.
  • Ignoring repeated calculation mistakes.
  • Spending too much time on one difficult question.
  • Avoiding topics that feel difficult.
  • Completing exercises without reviewing incorrect answers.
  • Trying to copy another student's timetable exactly.

Final thoughts

A good Class 10 Maths study plan does not need to be complicated. Start by understanding your current level, divide the syllabus into manageable targets, practise consistently and keep track of mistakes.

As examinations approach, gradually shift more of your time towards mixed practice, revision and timed papers. If you consistently struggle with particular concepts despite regular effort, additional academic support may be useful.

Most importantly, use your study plan as a guide rather than a rigid rule. Adjust it when your school schedule, progress or learning needs change.
